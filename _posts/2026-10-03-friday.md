Robinson Crusoe's ship has floundered off the coast of a desolate
island, he is the only survivor and he has nothing but his innate
skills and behaviors at his disposal. The good news is he is a genius
(+2) and can be focused (+1) from time to time. The bad news is he's
weak (0) and easily distracted (-1). In the game, these attributes
manifest via a starting deck of 18 cards.

You are not playing as Robinson Crusoe, though. You are Friday. But
not the Friday that Daniel Defoe wrote about. Not exactly. There's a
moment in <a
href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12623/pg12623-images.html">the
novel</a> that stands out to the reader:

> It happened one day about noon, going towards my boat, I was
> exceedingly surprised with the print of a man’s naked foot on the
> shore, which was very plain to be seen in the sand: I stood like one
> thunder-struck, or as if I had seen an apparition.

Years later Robinson rescues Friday from cannibals, so that's probably
not his footprint.  But what if it was? <i>Friday</i>, the game,
supposes that Friday, the man, secretly guides Robinson along his
journey of survival and learning. It starts with the choice of what to
do each day. Is Robinson ready to fight wild animals or should he
stick closer to his camp?

This choice is made by picking one of two cards pulled from another
deck that contains hazards of various levels. The easiest (labeled
slightly nonsensically "with the raft to the wreck") allows Robinson
to play a single card in order to overcome the challenge of recovering
something from the shipwreck. Initially even a weak Robinson can
accomplish the task since it only requires 0 fight. But when you go
through the hazard deck the second time the challenge is increased to
1 and the third time to 3. You can picture the surf breaking up the
wreck and carrying items into the depths. The other hazards are
"exploring the island", "further exploring the island", "wild
animals", and, of course, "cannibals". Each level has increasingly
difficult fight thresholds while allowing Robinson to freely play more
cards in order to defeat the hazard.

Beating the hazard allows you to take the hazard card and turn it 180°
making it a card for Robinson to add to his discard pile. Successfully
fighting the cannibals might reward him with a weapon that adds 4 to
his fighting strength. Scavenging the wreckage could allow Robinson to
return with equipment, books or food. Or he might gain a trait such as
deception, vision or experience. In addition to a fight number, many
of the added cards grant him special powers such as drawing free cards
or exchanging cards in play with new cards from the deck.

Now losing against a hazard isn't all bad news. You won't get the
extra card for your Robinson deck and you will lose life points
according to had badly you botched the job. But you also can cull
cards according to the number of life points you lost. Turning up a
distracted card when you set out on a raft turns out to be a decent
outcome because you can guarantee it won't ruin your day in the future
at a relatively small price. Some cards include a special ability to
destroy a card in play, so failing isn't the only way to remove
cards. But especially at the beginning failure is always an option and
a good way of eliminating some of Robinson's bad habits.

Friday's goal is to get Robinson in tip-top shape for the final
showdown against two ships of pirates. Working against that goal are
aging cards, which are added each time you exhaust the Robinson deck.
They start off bad and end worse. If you run out of aging cards,
Robinson dies of old age. You must also track Robinson's health via
life point tokens. Run out of those and he's overcome by injuries and
illness. Changes to the aging deck and initial life points allow
players to adjust the difficulty. That won't be necessary for a few
plays though.

<i>Friday</i>'s designer, Friedemann Friese, expertly balanced the
hazards so that Robinson rarely defeats them with the number of free
cards they specify. If you want to get the reward, you must spend a
life point token to play another card. If you are lucky, you'll beat
the hazard and take the win. Alternatively, you might pull a card that
makes it more efficient to lose and cull some junk from the deck. But
frequently you'll be put in the position of losing without a good
option and must once again decide whether to risk another life point
to turn another card. Especially during the third time through the
hazard deck, you risk falling into a trap where you can't win unless
you spend a life point.

It's a game where decisions start easy and progressively get
trickier. Good decisions hinge on knowing the state of the Robinson
deck. Since you've mostly built the deck yourself, that doesn't seem
too hard. But a stray aging card or a choice between equally
daunting hazards can put Robinson into a spiral he can't recover from.
<i>Friday</i> never feels unfair, but it's quick to punish a bad
decision. That makes winning all the sweeter.
